## Concepts and authors

- **Professional judgement**: interpreting, evaluating and acting when certainty is impossible; the opposite of applying a formula. Professional work involves ill-structured problems, shifting contexts, competing interpretations, and incomplete or uncertain information (Abbott, 1988).
- **Judgement as integration of forms of knowledge** (Blackler, 1995): technical expertise; tacit and experiential knowledge; ethical reasoning; contextual and organisational sensemaking. The deck lists encoded, encultured, embedded and embodied knowledge from Blackler's five forms.
- **Explicit and tacit knowledge** (Collins and Evans, 2007): explicit knowledge fits rules, textbooks and algorithms; tacit knowledge comes through practice, immersion and socialisation. Real expertise needs interactional expertise (ask the right questions, know what counts as evidence, grasp what practitioners find meaningful, judge in line with professional norms). Expertise is socially located: one must become a "native" of the practice.
- **The spy test**: a spy who learns a town from books and expatriates would fail a "Turing test" against a native because he lacks embodied experience, immersion and tacit cultural cues.
- **Jurisdiction and community** (Abbott, 1988): professional expertise lives in a professional community that controls a jurisdiction over tasks, problems and ways of reasoning; members learn judgement through socialisation.
- **Professional scepticism** in accounting and auditing: maintain objective judgement and do not accept client explanations without corroborating evidence; critically evaluate and question audit evidence; consider potential fraud or error risks; stay alert to contradictory information; challenge management assertions. There is a tension between efficiency and professional judgement (Gardner and Bryson, 2020).

## Technology in accounting and finance

- Trading moved from pits (bodily skill, voice, "market feel") through electronic order books to algorithmic and high-frequency trading, where speed, co-location and data processing decide competition. The Chicago Mercantile Exchange story illustrates how the basis of expertise shifted.
- Accounting: dashboards that aim to replace manual sampling, document query systems, verification, KYC and anti-money-laundering systems, fraud detection, ledger systems, automated transaction processing, compliance checks, predictive analytics.
- Finance: central order books, algorithmic trading, complex prediction models, alerting systems, cryptocurrency, custodian services; AI models with higher stated accuracy than traditional regression in the slide's illustration (88% against 29%; confirm the source before use).
- Trends: more data (including synthetic), more algorithms and complex models, cloud computing and large models, digital infrastructure, digital money, faster payments and settlement.
- Critical issues: lack of explainability, unclear decision logic and hidden biases in advanced models; infrastructure limits (distance, network speed, hardware, data-centre location, system integration, data quality, processing capacity, security); professional impact.
- Augmentation versus automation: an expert system couples a knowledge base and inference engine with a user interface through which users query and receive advice (Milana and Ashta, 2021).

## Auditing as the worked case (Samiolo et al., 2023 in the text; 2021 in the list, check)

- Audit work can be seen as routine, calculative and process driven (Frey and Osborne, 2017; Susskind and Susskind, 2015) and as a domain demanding individual discretion and subjective judgement (Power, 2003). Repetition and mechanical behaviours are intertwined with reflexive, sceptical attitudes.
- Structure versus judgement: automated working papers reduce reflexive writing and deliberation; standardised sampling limits exposure to transaction analysis; the "holistic view" develops less in early career.
- Auditors' voices on the slides: junior experience of "doing" is lost as low-level compliance work is automated, which threatens the experience for the whole system; and, from another auditor, technology should make more information available and enable alternative outcomes, which counters the bias of overlooking alternatives that regulators criticised.
- Challenges to scepticism: less direct exposure to client operations, overreliance on automated controls and analytics, limited development of judgement in juniors, harder spotting of unusual patterns, fewer chances to build deep client knowledge that informs scepticism.
- Trend: automation of core audit tasks, loss of foundational learning experiences, fewer opportunities for judgement, a shift from technical to relationship skills.

## Reading the debate in two columns

| Technology can enhance judgement | Technology can diminish judgement |
|---|---|
| Efficiency, speed, accuracy | Weaker interpretation |
| Huge data volume becomes usable | Less professional scepticism |
| More information to suggest alternative outcomes | Less contextual awareness |
| Frees time for relationship and advisory work | Erodes tacit knowledge and the micro-moments that build judgement |

The deck's recap: expertise is more than technique and has always evolved; professional legitimacy is challenged again; AI can make work faster while expertise gives work meaning and purpose; judgement grows in micro-moments; responsibility in the AI era means treating AI as a tool for thought and keeping the thinking human.

## Mediating ideas worth building an argument on

- Who holds the tacit knowledge and how do juniors acquire it when routine tasks disappear?
- Which tasks are routine and which need native judgement, and who decides the split?
- Hype cycle check: expectations such as "AI will replace accountants" meet high implementation costs, limited application areas, a continued need for human oversight and heavy infrastructure.
- Whether AI imitates expertise and still cannot be an expert (the lecture's conclusion on the native test, a thesis that students may test against evidence).
- Design choices: augmentation by design, human-in-the-loop checks, training that keeps juniors close to client operations.

## Coaching moves

- Ask the student to define professional judgement in their own words and give a case from accounting, finance or management.
- Ask for the strongest argument for the side they disagree with, with a source.
- Use the native test on a student-chosen scenario: write three questions (native, book learner, generic) and explain what separates them.
- Have the student map each argument to a source they have read in full.
