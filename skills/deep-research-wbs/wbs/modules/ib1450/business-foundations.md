## What is a business

- Students' chat definitions: sells goods or services, meets needs, generates money, collection of people with a common goal, value-generating activity, a for-profit or other organisation, a reporting entity, a separate legal entity with perpetual succession.
- Oxford English Dictionary: "trade and all activity relating to it, especially considered in terms of volume or profitability; commercial transactions, engagements and undertakings regarded collectively... more generally, the world of trade and commerce; an organisation that sells products and or services." No mention of profit or shareholders in the core sense.
- Peter Drucker: before profit, the organisation has to satisfy the customer or meet the customer's need. Combine making profit with meeting needs.
- Quiz hint from the lecturers: when asked to define something, avoid recycling the name of the thing inside the definition.

## Purposes and roles of business

Two families: money and society.

- Money: maximise profit; maximise shareholder value (increase the value of the shares and of equity, pay dividends); innovation; long-run versus short-run (Amazon accepted early losses to scale and outcompete rivals).
- Society: jobs, community development, good service, ethical responsibility, taxes, social welfare, avoiding harm to the environment.
- Friedman is the classic voice for shareholder value (the lecturer called it "slightly problematic now"). Conceptual contrast between shareholder primacy and stakeholder approaches is a likely essay frame.
- Service-profit chain (operations management literature): happy workers lead to happier, more satisfied customers.
- Henry Ford's efficiency wages: pay a multiple of going wages to attract and keep the best workers and let them afford the product; the work remained hard.
- Ryanair: softened its treatment of customers after learning that harshness at the gate hurt the business.
- Poor owners and monopolies: Thames Water shown as an example of a monopoly service that faces renationalisation pressure (lecturer's view; verify).

## Stakeholders

Owners, employees, customers, shareholders, suppliers, society. A business depends on all of them for its operations. Ignoring or squeezing one can damage the business in the long term. Engage with all of them constructively.

## Functional areas

| Area | Lecture's description |
|---|---|
| Operations | The set of processes that transform inputs (physical goods, services or data) into something useful; resources may sit in suppliers or in a factory |
| Marketing | Understanding and stimulating demand (promotion, research, brand, store design) |
| Accounting and finance | Understanding performance through lagging indicators, and financing the business; disclosure when a company goes public |
| Management, leadership and human resources | Dealing with the members of the organisation so that they contribute at their best; running the organisation. A student added IT as a function worth listing |

Zara (Inditex) as a teaching case across the functions:

- Marketing: little traditional advertising; store design and the weekly flow of new items; many small collections tested in shops and fast scaling of what sells (the lecturers note they watch runways, celebrities and street fashion).
- Operations: very short lead times; real-time digital connection to shops; cloth cut in-house; sewing sent to nearby suppliers; items flown to shops about twice a week; stock turns over roughly every two weeks; "when it's gone, it's gone".
- Management and HR: large headquarters near A Coruna; family leadership; designers and prototyping in one place.
- Finance: expanded through its own cash generation, little debt; disclosure expanded on listing. Contrast with retailers that failed after heavy borrowing.
- The flip side: environmental cost of fast fashion, a system issue.

## External environment: PESTLE

Political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental. Apple illustration from the lecture: tariffs, sanctions, geopolitics, access to components and raw materials (political); taxes, interest rates, GDP, consumer confidence (economic); worker well-being at assemblers, reparability and planned obsolescence (social); dependence on a few chip makers and lithography suppliers (technological); standard charger regulation and GDPR (legal); conflict minerals (environmental).

## AI and the anatomy of business

Lecturer's view: AI will not transform the fundamental functions. Businesses may specialise more in particular parts. Two patterns of use: narrow specialised systems (for example tumour identification in radiography) and general tools handed to professionals to automate parts of their work. Most organisations do not understand their own processes in detail, so adding AI to improve them is hard.

## Self-test (use in Concept coach mode, one question at a time)

1. Define "business" without reusing the word. Where does your definition place profit?
2. Give one argument for shareholder value and one for stakeholder value, each with an example and a limit.
3. Explain what operations does for a services company.
4. Which function would first notice a fall in demand, and what would it do?
5. Walk through PESTLE for a sector of your choice with one item per letter.
6. Why does the Zara model depend on fast information flow from stores?
7. State the three pillars and give an example of tension and an example of synergy between two of them.
