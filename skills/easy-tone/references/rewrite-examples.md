## Running example: Mia's Lemonade Stand

Use Mia whenever the book uses a firm or a hypothetical business. Keep the numbers small and round.

| Concept | Mia version |
|---|---|
| Owner puts money in | Mia puts $100 of her savings in the cash jar |
| Buying on credit with a note | Mia buys a juice squeezer for $60 and promises to pay in 3 months |
| Money before the job | A neighbor pays $12 today for lemonade Mia will deliver next week |
| Expense paid in cash | Mia pays $9 for the stand's spot this month |
| Prepaid expense | Mia pays $12 for a year of insurance on day one |
| Accrued expense | Her helper worked 4 days and gets paid next week |
| Depreciation | The $60 squeezer lasts 60 months, so it costs $1 per month |

## 1. Objective of financial reporting (p. 64)

**Textbook:** "provide financial information about the reporting entity that is useful to present and potential equity investors, lenders, and other creditors in making decisions"

**Easy:** The goal of a company's reports is to give useful money facts. People who might put money in, or lend money, read them. They use the facts to decide what to do.

**In exam words:** The objective of general-purpose financial reporting is to provide financial information about the reporting entity that is useful to present and potential equity investors, lenders and other creditors in making decisions about providing resources to the entity.

## 2. Neutrality (p. 127)

**Textbook:** "Neutrality means that a company cannot select information to favor one set of interested parties over another."

**Easy:** **Neutral** means no favorites. A company can't pick facts that help one group and hide facts that help another.

**Picture the book uses:** People bet on boxing because they trust the fights are real. Nobody bets on wrestling because the public thinks it is rigged. Reports need the same trust, or nobody uses them.

**In exam words:** Neutrality means a company cannot select information to favor one set of interested parties over another.

## 3. Prudence (p. 127)

**Textbook:** "Prudence is the exercise of caution when making judgments under conditions of uncertainty."

**Easy:** **Prudence** means being careful when you are not sure. You don't make assets and income look bigger than they are. You don't make debts and costs look smaller than they are.

## 4. Fair value (pp. 143-145)

**Textbook:** the price "that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date"

**Easy:** **Fair value** is what you could sell something for today in a fair deal. Think of a rare trading card. You paid $10, and today the shop pays $50. The fair value is $50.

**Three levels (the book's hierarchy):**
- Level 1: a posted price you can look up, like a share price in the newspaper
- Level 2: prices of similar things being sold
- Level 3: nobody sells anything like it, so you make your best guess

**In exam words:** Fair value is the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date.

## 5. Revenue recognition (pp. 147-148)

**Textbook:** "recognize revenue in the accounting period in which the performance obligation is satisfied"

**Easy:** **Revenue recognition** means you write down the money you earned in the time period when you did the job.

**Example:** Mia delivers lemonade on June 30. The neighbor pays in July. Mia writes the revenue down in June. At the end of June the books show a **receivable**: money Mia expects to collect.

**Real example:** Airbus signs a deal to sell planes to British Airways for 100 million euros. Airbus writes the revenue down when the planes are delivered.

**The five steps (Illustration 2.5):**
1. Find the contract (the agreement)
2. Find the promises in it
3. Set the price
4. Split the price across the promises
5. Write the revenue down when each promise is kept

## 6. Expense recognition (p. 149)

**Textbook:** "Let the expense follow the revenues."

**Easy:** Write a cost down in the same period as the money it helped earn. Mia buys lemons on Monday and sells the lemonade on Wednesday. The lemon cost goes in the books on Wednesday.

**Two kinds of cost:**
| Kind | Meaning | Example | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product cost | Goes into what you sell | Lemons, sugar, cups | In the period you sell it |
| Period cost | Has no clear link to one sale | The boss's salary | Right away |

## 7. Recording a transaction: the six-row panel (pp. 221-229)

Kieso analyses every transaction in six rows. Keep the six rows and answer each in one plain sentence.

**Mia gets money before the job (like Illustration 3.12):**

| Row | Answer |
|---|---|
| Transaction | A neighbor pays Mia $12 today for lemonade to be delivered next week |
| Basic analysis | The cash jar grows by $12. Mia now owes a job, so a debt called **unearned service revenue** grows by $12 |
| Equation analysis | Assets +$12 equals liabilities +$12. The equation stays balanced |
| Debit-credit analysis | Debits make assets grow, so debit Cash. Credits make liabilities grow, so credit Unearned Service Revenue |
| Journal entry | See below |
| Post to ledger | Copy the $12 to the Cash page (left side) and to the Unearned Service Revenue page (right side) |

```
Cash                         12
    Unearned Service Revenue     12
```

**The side rule:** the left side (debit) makes assets and expenses grow. The right side (credit) makes liabilities, equity and revenue grow.

**Why unearned revenue is a liability (the book's point):** Many liabilities have "payable" in the name. Unearned revenue has none, and it still counts as a debt, because Mia owes a job.

## 8. Adjusting entries (pp. 231-232)

**Textbook:** "In order for revenues to be recorded in the period in which services are performed and for expenses to be recognized in the period in which they are incurred, companies make adjusting entries."

**Easy:** At the end of the period, the books need a fix. Some events never got written down. Adjusting entries make the reports show the true picture. They make sure revenue and expense land in the right period.

**Why the trial balance can be out of date (the book's three reasons):**
1. Some events are not written down every day. Using up supplies is one.
2. Some costs expire as time passes, like rent, insurance and wear on equipment.
3. Some items are not recorded yet, like a utility bill that arrives next month.

**The four kinds (Illustration 3.21):**

| Group | Kind | Easy meaning | Mia example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deferral | Prepaid expense | Paid in advance, used later | $12 for a year of insurance |
| Deferral | Unearned revenue | Money in, job not done | The neighbor's $12 |
| Accrual | Accrued revenue | Job done, money not in | Lemonade delivered, bill not paid |
| Accrual | Accrued expense | Cost used, bill not paid | Helper worked 4 days, paid next week |

**Test to tell them apart:** Ask which came first, the cash or the event. Cash first means deferral. Event first means accrual.

**Insurance adjustment:**

```
Insurance Expense            1
    Prepaid Insurance            1
```

Mia paid $12 for 12 months. One month passed, so $1 moves from the asset (Prepaid Insurance) to an expense.

## 9. Depreciation (p. 236)

**Textbook idea:** A company records a building as an asset in the year it buys it, because the building gives service for many years. That time is its **useful life**.

**Easy:** **Depreciation** means spreading the cost of something over the years you use it. Mia's $60 squeezer lasts 60 months. Each month Mia writes $1 of its cost down as an expense.

```
Depreciation Expense         1
    Accumulated Depreciation     1
```

## 10. Standard-setters (pp. 67-77)

**Easy:** Companies in many countries follow the same rulebook, **IFRS**. A group called the **IASB** writes it. The IASB has no police power. Other regulators in each country make companies follow it. The European Union, for example, requires listed companies to use IFRS.

**Due process (p. 72):** The steps to make a new rule are open and public. Many groups give comments before the rule is final.

**Which rule to look at first (p. 77):**
1. The IFRS standards and their interpretations
2. The Conceptual Framework
3. Rules from other standard-setters with a similar framework
